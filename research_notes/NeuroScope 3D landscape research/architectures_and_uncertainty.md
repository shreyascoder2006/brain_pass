# BraTS Architectures/Benchmarks & Uncertainty Quantification

## What are the top-performing architectures on BraTS 2020-2024, with Dice/HD95 for WT/TC/ET, and training data/compute requirements?

### Takeaway
nnU-Net (and its BraTS-specific variants/ensembles) has been the dominant baseline/winner lineage since BraTS 2020, with Dice in the high-80s to low-90s and HD95 ranging widely (single digits to >20mm) depending on region and postprocessing; MedNeXt-based ensembles are the leading 2023-2024 architecture family (often ensembled with nnU-Net/SegResNet/SwinUNETR); transformer-only models (Swin UNETR, UNETR++) are competitive but not clearly superior to CNN-based nnU-Net/MedNeXt on BraTS, and most winning entries are multi-model ensembles with heavy postprocessing rather than a single novel architecture.

### Cited Findings
- nnU-Net won BraTS 2020: final ensemble achieved Dice 88.95 (WT), 85.06 (TC), 82.03 (ET); HD95 8.498 (WT), 17.337 (TC), 17.805 (ET) — [nnU-Net for Brain Tumor Segmentation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.00848)
- The nnU-Net paper states the unmodified baseline nnU-Net configuration already achieves a "respectable result," with further gains from BraTS-specific postprocessing, region-based training, and more aggressive augmentation — [nnU-Net for Brain Tumor Segmentation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.00848)
- A third-party BraTS 2021 comparison table reports SegResNet at ~0.891 average Dice / ~5.78mm average HD95 vs. nnU-Net at ~0.879 average Dice / ~12.18mm average HD95; nnU-Net had the best WT HD95 (3.55mm) but much worse ET HD95 (22.44mm), driving its worse average — [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12084721/table/t3) (source does not specify validation vs. test split or identical training settings)
- A BraTS 2021 ensemble of modified TransBTS + nnU-Net reported test-set Dice (ET, TC, WT order) 0.8789, 0.8759, 0.9279 and HD95 10.426, 17.203, 4.93 — [Ensemble CNN Networks for GBM Tumors Segmentation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.06554)
- A separate BraTS 2021 team reported the best average HD95 of 8.7886 with average DSC of 0.8739 — [HNF-Netv2 for Brain Tumor Segmentation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05268) (as cited via search; exact attribution unclear, treat cautiously)
- On BraTS 2023 GLA validation, nnU-Net achieved average Dice 0.8778 / average HD95 10.09mm; a newer HT-CNNs transfer-learning model reached average DSC 0.8842 (on par with the 2023 winner, higher than 2020-2022 winners) and average HD95 9.43mm, described as improving over 2021-2023 winners — [Unified HT-CNNs Architecture](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.08240)
- A MedNeXt+SegResNet approach placed third in the BraTS 2023 Adult Glioma Challenge with average Dice 0.8313 and average HD95 36.38 on the test set — [arXiv 2412.08240 context / BraTS 2023 results reporting](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.08240)
- A recent BraTS 2023 GLI paper reports mean Dice 0.897 (ET 0.859, TC 0.902, WT 0.931) with HD95 below 5.2mm on the held-out test set (no nnU-Net/SegResNet baseline given in the excerpt found) — [SegGuidedNet](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.22572)
- BraTS 2024 SSA + Pediatric tumor task: MedNeXt-based (MBZUAI) model achieved average Dice 0.896 (SSA) / 0.830 (Pediatric); average HD95 14.682 (SSA) / 37.508 (Pediatric) on unseen validation — [Optimizing Brain Tumor Segmentation with MedNeXt](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.15872)
- BraTS-GoAT challenge: same group's MedNeXt-based ensemble with postprocessing reached average DSC 85.54% / HD95 27.88 on unseen validation — [Optimizing Brain Tumor Segmentation with MedNeXt](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.15872)
- EMedNeXt (MedNeXt V2) on BraTS-SSA hidden validation: lesion-wise Dice 0.845 (ET), 0.860 (TC), 0.914 (WT); fine-tuned version 0.870, 0.863, 0.919 (NSD reported, not HD95) — [EMedNeXt](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.23256)
- A BraTS-Africa comparison: fine-tuned MedNeXt average legacy Dice 0.887 vs. fine-tuned nnU-Net 0.889 (near-parity); HD95 comparison not retrieved — [Brain Tumor Segmentation in Sub-Sahara Africa with Advanced Transformer and ConvNet Methods](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.10905)
- UNETR++ (transformer-based, efficient) reports on the Synapse multi-organ dataset (not BraTS): UNETR DSC 78.35%, Swin-UNETR DSC 83.48%, UNETR++ DSC 87.22%, while reducing parameters/FLOPs by over 71% vs. nnFormer — [UNETR++ paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.04497) (note: this is Synapse, not BraTS — no direct BraTS Dice/HD95 for UNETR++ was found in this search)
- A multi-task BraTS 2023/2024 benchmark found Swin UNETR has ~62.2M parameters with inference times ~1.40-1.45s per case; plain 3D U-Net was fastest at ~0.20-0.25s per case but with lowest accuracy — [A Unified Benchmark of Deep Learning Models for Multi-task 3D Brain Tumor Segmentation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.28858)
- BraTS 2024 challenge expanded to post-treatment glioma segmentation (new task distinct from pre-treatment GLI); official results in the challenge paper — [The 2024 BraTS Challenge: Glioma Segmentation on Post-treatment MRI](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.18368)
- Secondary (non-primary) source states a group that also won BraTS 2023 pre-treatment (ensembling SwinUNETR + nnU-Net, trained on real + GAN-augmented data) took first place in BraTS 2024 GLI post-treatment; team name not confirmed in retrieved text — reported via search summary, no direct URL confirmed as primary source
- BraTS 2024 SSA (sub-Saharan Africa) leading approach: pretrained on the large GLI-pre cohort then fine-tuned on the smaller SSA dataset using a MedNeXt-nnU-Net ensemble — reported via search summary (same context as above)

### Inferences
- The overall architecture trend 2020→2024 is: nnU-Net-family CNNs remain a strong, hard-to-beat baseline; winning BraTS entries increasingly rely on ensembling multiple architectures (nnU-Net, SegResNet, SwinUNETR, MedNeXt) plus aggressive postprocessing/data augmentation rather than a single architectural breakthrough.
- Pure transformer architectures (Swin UNETR, UNETR++) show competitive accuracy but not a clear Dice advantage over CNN-based nnU-Net/MedNeXt on BraTS specifically, while costing more compute (Swin UNETR: more parameters, ~5-7x slower per-case than plain 3D U-Net).
- Direct cross-paper Dice/HD95 comparison is inherently noisy: results differ by BraTS year, region-wise vs. lesion-wise metrics, validation vs. test split, and single-model vs. ensemble — any report should flag this caveat when giving numbers.

### Gaps
- No official, single authoritative BraTS 2021, 2023, or 2024 leaderboard table (per-architecture, per-region Dice/HD95) was retrieved directly from the official BraTS/Synapse leaderboard or MICCAI proceedings — only third-party comparison tables and individual team papers, which may use inconsistent splits/settings.
- BraTS 2025 leaderboard/winner results were not found in this search.
- Precise training data/compute requirements (GPU-hours, number of GPUs, training time) for nnU-Net, MedNeXt, Swin UNETR, UNETR++ on BraTS were not found in retrieved snippets — would require reading full papers' training/implementation sections.
- Direct BraTS-specific Dice/HD95 numbers for UNETR++ (its own Table 5 on BraTS) were not retrieved; only its Synapse multi-organ numbers were found. Would need to fetch arXiv 2212.04497 directly for the BraTS table.

## What is MONAI's pretrained "brats_mri_segmentation" bundle (SegResNet, BraTS 2018) — Dice performance, and comparison to 2021+ methods? Is it a legitimate real-time baseline or outdated?

### Takeaway
The MONAI bundle is a SegResNet-style model trained only on the small BraTS 2018 dataset (285 cases, split 200/42/43); a third-party listing (unverified against MONAI's own docs) cites ~85.18% average Dice on BraTS 2018, which is roughly 3-10 Dice points below 2021+ SOTA methods (nnU-Net ~87-89%, MedNeXt-ensembles ~88-90%) — it is a legitimate lightweight/fast inference demo baseline but is clearly outdated relative to current SOTA and was never validated on BraTS 2021+ data.

### Cited Findings
- The bundle is described as a pretrained model for 3D segmentation of brain tumor subregions (ET, TC, WT) from 4-channel multimodal MRI (T1c, T1, T2, FLAIR), based on BraTS 2018 data — [NGC Catalog — monai_brats_mri_segmentation](https://catalog.ngc.nvidia.com/orgs/nvidia/monaitoolkit/models/monai_brats_mri_segmentation/-)
- Labeled data: 285 3D volumes (4 channels each) from BraTS 2018, split into 200 training / 42 validation / 43 test studies — [NGC Catalog](https://catalog.ngc.nvidia.com/orgs/nvidia/monaitoolkit/models/monai_brats_mri_segmentation/0.5.1)
- Training approach described as similar to "3D MRI brain tumor segmentation using autoencoder regularization," a winning method in BraTS 2018; training config uses SegResNet, 300 epochs, Adam, LR 1e-4, Dice loss, AMP enabled, blocks_down [1,2,2,4], init_filters 16 — [MONAI/brats_mri_segmentation README](https://huggingface.co/MONAI/brats_mri_segmentation/blob/0.5.3/docs/README.md)
- A third-party listing reports an 85.18% average Dice score across tumor subregions on BraTS 2018 — [PromptLayer model listing](https://www.promptlayer.com/models/bratsmrisegmentation) (flagged explicitly as unverified against NVIDIA's/MONAI's own published numbers, which only show a validation Dice curve over 300 epochs without a stated final number in the retrieved docs)
- Latest bundle version on NGC is 0.5.2; Hugging Face mirror shows 0.5.3, referencing monai_version 0.9.0 and pytorch_version 1.10.0 — compatibility with current MONAI/PyTorch should be checked — [HuggingFace MONAI/brats_mri_segmentation blame](https://huggingface.co/MONAI/brats_mri_segmentation/blame/main/docs/README.md)

### Inferences
- Comparing the ~85.18% (unverified) BraTS-2018 Dice figure for the MONAI bundle against the Part-A findings above (nnU-Net ~87-89% average Dice on BraTS 2020/2021, MedNeXt-ensembles ~88-90% on BraTS 2023/2024), the MONAI bundle sits roughly 3-5+ Dice points below modern SOTA — a real but moderate gap, not catastrophic, consistent with "legitimate baseline, clearly outdated vs. SOTA."
- Because the bundle was trained and tested only on BraTS 2018 (a smaller, less diverse cohort than BraTS 2021+), and general findings on cross-year/cross-center domain shift show consistent accuracy drops when a BraTS-year-specific model is applied to a different year's data without fine-tuning (see cross-reference below), the bundle's real-world Dice on BraTS 2021+ held-out cases is likely lower than its own BraTS-2018 Dice — i.e., a hackathon team should expect degraded performance from this exact bundle on 2021+ data.
- As an inference/demo baseline (not accuracy benchmark), the bundle is reasonable: it is lightweight, fast, off-the-shelf, and requires no training — appropriate for a hackathon's "working pipeline" demonstration, but should not be presented as SOTA-equivalent.

### Gaps
- No official MONAI/NVIDIA-published final test-set Dice number for the bundle was found in the retrieved text (only a training-curve description) — the 85.18% figure is third-party and unverified; flag this clearly to the report writer.
- No direct study was found that runs the exact MONAI brats_mri_segmentation bundle (unmodified, no fine-tuning) on BraTS 2021 held-out data to report a precise degraded Dice number — this specific gap is addressed qualitatively below (next question) via general domain-shift literature, not a direct measurement.

## Are there architectures specifically optimized for fast inference (<2 min/case on T4-class GPU) vs. compute-heavy ones? What inference times are benchmarked?

### Takeaway
Plain 3D U-Net and SegResNet-style CNNs are the fastest BraTS architectures (well under a minute per case even on datacenter GPUs), transformer-heavy models like Swin UNETR are markedly slower (over a second per case on strong GPUs, scaling to multiple minutes on lower-tier hardware) but still generally feasible within a 2-minute budget, while UNETR++ was specifically designed to cut compute/parameters vs. transformer baselines while remaining accurate; however, no source gave T4-specific benchmark numbers, so any T4 time estimate requires extrapolation.

### Cited Findings
- A multi-task BraTS 2023/2024 benchmark reports 3D U-Net as the most computationally efficient architecture tested, with inference times of ~0.20-0.25s per patient (lowest accuracy of tested models) — [A Unified Benchmark of Deep Learning Models for Multi-task 3D Brain Tumor Segmentation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.28858)
- The same benchmark reports Swin UNETR at 62.2M parameters with inference times of ~1.40-1.45s per patient — [A Unified Benchmark of Deep Learning Models for Multi-task 3D Brain Tumor Segmentation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.28858)
- NVIDIA's MONAI Swin UNETR bundle reports ~124ms model-only compute time on an A100 80GB GPU in PyTorch, dropping to ~43ms with TensorRT FP16 — [MONAI/swin_unetr_btcv_segmentation docs via NGC catalog] (this is BTCV organ segmentation, not BraTS, and A100 is a notably stronger GPU than T4)
- UNETR++ is explicitly designed for efficiency: it reduces parameters/FLOPs by over 71% vs. nnFormer on Synapse while improving DSC from 83.48% (Swin-UNETR) to 87.22% — [UNETR++ paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.04497)
- A UNETR++ BraTS benchmark protocol (measured on a Quadro RTX 6000 24GB GPU + 32-core Xeon CPU, 128×128×128 inputs) states UNETR++ "runs at a faster inference speed than recent transformer-based methods while needing significantly less GPU memory," but exact millisecond/second values were not retrieved from the search snippet — [patent document reproducing UNETR++ comparison](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12373956)

### Inferences
- Given the figures above (3D U-Net ~0.2-0.25s/case, Swin UNETR ~1.4s/case on unspecified-but-presumably-strong GPUs), both families are comfortably within a 2-minute-per-case T4 budget even allowing for T4 being ~2-4x slower than an A100/RTX6000-class GPU for inference — the actual full pipeline time (preprocessing, I/O, model loading) is likely to dominate the 2-minute budget more than raw architecture forward-pass time for any single-model inference without heavy TTA/ensembling.
- Architectures explicitly marketed/designed for efficiency (3D U-Net, SegResNet, UNETR++) are the safer choice for a hackathon's fast-inference constraint; full ensembles (nnU-Net multi-fold, MedNeXt ensembles used in BraTS-winning entries) likely multiply inference time by the number of ensemble members and may be harder to fit in 2 minutes on a T4, though no source gives a direct ensemble-time benchmark.

### Gaps
- No source reports inference time benchmarked specifically on a T4 GPU for any BraTS architecture — all found benchmarks use A100, RTX 6000, or unspecified/generic GPUs. T4-specific timing would need to be measured directly or extrapolated using relative GPU throughput (T4 FP16 TFLOPS vs. A100/RTX6000).
- No source reports total end-to-end pipeline time (preprocessing + inference + postprocessing) for a full BraTS case, only raw model forward-pass time in most cases.

## What uncertainty quantification methods exist for BraTS/brain tumor segmentation (TTA, deep ensembles, MC dropout, evidential DL, conformal prediction)? Cite specific papers and findings.

### Takeaway
Five method families are established in BraTS-specific or closely related literature: test-time augmentation (TTA) for aleatoric uncertainty, test-time dropout (TTD)/MC dropout for epistemic uncertainty, ensembles (often combined with TTA/TTD), evidential deep learning (EDL) as a cheaper single-pass alternative, and conformal prediction (mostly 2024+, not yet BraTS-specific in most found papers but applied to biomedical segmentation generally) for distribution-free guarantees; TTA-based approaches were used in actual BraTS Challenge submissions (BraTS 2017, 2020) while EDL and conformal prediction are presented as improvements addressing the high computational cost of ensembles/MC-dropout.

### Cited Findings
- Wang et al. augmented test images with 3D rotation, flipping, scaling, and noise to get aleatoric uncertainty via TTA, contrasted with model-based (epistemic) uncertainty from test-time dropout; their method was part of a second-rank BraTS 2017 entry and found the uncertainty "can indicate potential mis-segmentations" — [Aleatoric uncertainty estimation with test-time augmentation for medical image segmentation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1807.07356) and [Automatic Brain Tumor Segmentation using CNNs with Test-Time Augmentation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.07884)
- Wang et al. noted a key limitation: more complex TTA transformations such as elastic deformations take longer to test and have lower efficiency, and suggested combining ensembles or test-time dropout with TTA for fuller uncertainty coverage — [Automatic Brain Tumor Segmentation using CNNs with Test-Time Augmentation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.07884)
- Mora Ballestar & Vilaplana built an ensemble of 3D encoder-decoder models (patch-based) with voxel-wise uncertainty from test-time dropout (epistemic) and TTA (aleatoric), used in the actual BraTS'20 Challenge Task 1 (segmentation) and Task 3 (uncertainty estimation) — [MRI brain tumor segmentation and uncertainty estimation using 3D-UNet architectures](https://deepai.org/publication/mri-brain-tumor-segmentation-and-uncertainty-estimation-using-3d-unet-architectures)
- Li, Nan, Del Ser & Yang (2022) proposed region-based Evidential Deep Learning (EDL) for BraTS, arguing existing approaches (ensembles, MC dropout, quantile regression, Bayesian NNs) are "limited by their high computational cost and inconsistency"; they use Subjective Logic to parameterize network outputs as a Dirichlet distribution, tested on BraTS 2020, reporting top performance in quantifying uncertainty and robustness while keeping low computational cost — [Region-based evidential deep learning to quantify uncertainty and improve robustness of brain tumor segmentation](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10505106/)
- A Neurocomputing-style paper compares test-time dropout and TTA for 3D brain tumor segmentation using N=40 Monte Carlo passes, visualized with a purple(low)-to-yellow(high) uncertainty color scale — found via search (exact arXiv ID not directly captured; cross-reference with Part B visualization question below)
- Conformal prediction for biomedical/medical image segmentation is a 2024+ development, not yet established specifically for BraTS in the papers found: "Conformal confidence sets for biomedical image segmentation" (Davenport, arXiv 2410.03406, late 2024) applies conformal inference at the pixel level (demonstrated on polyp detection, not BraTS) — [arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03406](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03406)
- Several conformal-prediction papers explicitly frame their work against MC-Dropout/ensembles, noting those heuristic methods "provide useful uncertainty maps but do not yield distribution-free guarantees at deployment," a stated motivation for conformal methods — reported across several 2024-2026 papers including COMPASS (arXiv 2509.22240) and SACP (MIDL 2026)
- A 2025 empirical study on 2D brain tumor segmentation found MC-Dropout uncertainty correlates only weakly with boundary segmentation errors (global correlation r≈0.30-0.38; boundary correlation |r|<0.05), concluding MC Dropout "provides limited cues for boundary error localization" and suggesting hybrid methods — [An Empirical Study on MC Dropout-Based Uncertainty-Error Correlation in 2D Brain Tumor Segmentation](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15541v1)
- A 2026 preprint proposes "Variance-Penalized MC-Dropout as a Learned Smoothing Prior" specifically for brain tumour segmentation, indicating continued active development of MC-dropout variants for this exact task — [Variance-Penalized MC-Dropout as a Learned Smoothing Prior for Brain Tumour Segmentation](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.08956v1)

### Inferences
- The field has moved from pure TTA/ensemble approaches (2017-2020, used in actual BraTS submissions) toward cheaper single-pass alternatives (EDL, 2022) and toward formally-guaranteed approaches (conformal prediction, 2024+) as the main lines of improvement, largely motivated by the same complaint: ensembles/MC-dropout are computationally expensive and their raw uncertainty doesn't reliably localize actual errors (as shown by the 2025 boundary-correlation study).
- For a compute/time-constrained hackathon pipeline, this trajectory suggests EDL (single forward pass, Dirichlet-based) and/or a small/cheap TTA (e.g., flip-only, 2-4 augmentations) are more practical choices than full MC-dropout (many passes) or multi-model ensembles, though no single paper directly benchmarks "cheapest viable UQ method under a 2-minute budget" for BraTS.

### Gaps
- No BraTS-specific conformal prediction paper was found in this search (the conformal prediction results found are general biomedical segmentation, e.g., polyps, pancreatic tumor, not brain tumor/BraTS specifically) — this is a real gap in the literature as surveyed, not just a search miss, based on how the found papers frame their novelty.
- Exact Dice/uncertainty-quality numbers for the Li et al. EDL paper (vs. ensemble/MC-dropout baselines on BraTS 2020) were not retrieved — only the abstract-level claims of "top performance." Full paper would need to be read for quantitative comparison tables.

## How is uncertainty visualized/communicated (heatmaps, boundary bands, scalar scores, calibration plots)? Which are lightweight enough for a ~2-minute inference budget?

### Takeaway
Voxel-wise uncertainty heatmaps (entropy/variance color-coded, often purple-to-yellow or black-to-white) overlaid on the MRI slice are the dominant visualization in BraTS-related UQ papers, frequently paired with a 4-panel figure (input image, predicted vs. ground-truth contour, probability map, uncertainty map); of the methods surveyed, a small/cheap TTA (few augmentations) or evidential deep learning (single forward pass) are the only approaches clearly compatible with a tight 2-minute budget, while MC-dropout (typically N=40+ passes) and full deep ensembles are comparatively expensive and harder to guarantee within that budget without careful engineering.

### Cited Findings
- The UPMAD-Net paper (BraTS, MC dropout) shows a four-panel figure: FLAIR image, predicted vs. ground-truth contours, a probability map, and an uncertainty heatmap ranging from black (low uncertainty) to white (high uncertainty), explicitly highlighting tumor boundary regions as having elevated uncertainty — [UPMAD-Net: A Brain Tumor Segmentation Network with Uncertainty Guidance and Adaptive Multimodal Feature Fusion](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.03494)
- A 3D brain tumor TTD-vs-TTA comparison paper visualizes uncertainty maps using N=40 Monte Carlo passes with a purple(low)-to-yellow(high) color bar overlay — found via search summary (specific paper/URL not fully captured in this pass; consistent with the TTD/TTA uncertainty literature pattern noted above)
- Wang et al.'s TTA uncertainty work computes voxel-wise uncertainty (variance across augmented predictions) specifically to indicate potential mis-segmentations, i.e., using the heatmap as an error-flagging tool rather than just a confidence display — [Automatic Brain Tumor Segmentation using CNNs with Test-Time Augmentation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.07884)
- A 2025 lesion-uncertainty paper trains an auxiliary graph network on top of voxel-wise MC-dropout uncertainty (entropy, variance) to produce a per-lesion (rather than per-voxel) uncertainty score, moving from a raw heatmap toward a single summarized scalar per lesion — [Beyond Voxel Prediction Uncertainty: Identifying brain lesions you can trust](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.10877)
- The Li et al. EDL paper produces "uncertainty maps alongside the segmentation" directly from the Dirichlet-distribution parameterization in a single forward pass, explicitly noted as retaining "low computational cost and easy implementation," i.e., no repeated sampling needed — [Region-based evidential deep learning](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10505106/)
- Wang et al. explicitly flagged a speed/complexity tradeoff within TTA itself: simple transforms (flip, rotation) are efficient, while complex ones (elastic deformation) "take longer time for testing and have a lower efficiency" — [Automatic Brain Tumor Segmentation using CNNs with Test-Time Augmentation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.07884)
- The 2025 MC-dropout boundary-correlation study (|r|<0.05 for boundary errors) implies that, even when lightweight, pure MC-dropout heatmaps may not be trustworthy for flagging actual boundary mistakes, limiting their communicative value even if computed cheaply — [An Empirical Study on MC Dropout-Based Uncertainty-Error Correlation](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15541v1)

### Inferences
- Ranking by likely compute cost within a 2-minute budget, from cheapest to most expensive: (1) EDL / single-pass Dirichlet uncertainty (one forward pass, no extra cost) > (2) small TTA with 2-4 cheap augmentations (flip/rotation only, a few extra forward passes) > (3) MC-dropout with reduced N (e.g., N=10-20 instead of 40) > (4) full MC-dropout (N=40) or small ensembles (2-3 models) > (5) large multi-fold ensembles (5+ models) used in top BraTS Challenge entries — the last two are likely infeasible to combine with a single sub-2-minute inference pass unless running multiple GPUs in parallel.
- No paper found explicitly benchmarks "uncertainty computation wall-clock time" as a separate reported metric; this is inferred from the number of forward passes/augmentations each method requires, not from a direct timing measurement.

### Gaps
- No paper directly reports wall-clock time added by its specific UQ method (e.g., "+X seconds for TTA with N=8") in a BraTS or near-BraTS segmentation context — this would need to be measured empirically for a specific pipeline/hardware.
- Calibration plots (reliability diagrams) specifically for BraTS segmentation uncertainty were not found in this search; the literature retrieved favors voxel-heatmap visualization over calibration-curve style communication. This may be a genuine gap in the brain-tumor-specific UQ literature, or simply not surfaced by the queries used.

## Is there an established method for flagging out-of-distribution inputs (wrong modality, non-skull-stripped, non-brain) in brain MRI segmentation pipelines? Any lightweight techniques usable in a fast pipeline?

### Takeaway
No single standard method is established, but the literature shows: (1) DICOM-header-based modality checks are known to be unreliable because headers are often mis-filled, so an image-based check is needed; (2) a 2025 normalizing-flow approach is a concrete, benchmarked, post-hoc OOD detector tested explicitly on modality-shift (FLAIR/T1ce) and clear-outlier (CT) cases using BraTS 2020 + CQ500 data; (3) uncertainty-based OOD detection using only the segmentation model's own confidence is reported to fail in some settings (MS lesion study) unless combined with anatomical/auxiliary-label learning; (4) deterministic uncertainty methods (DUM) analyzing intermediate activations outperformed simple maximum-softmax-probability (MSP) baselines in a brain-structure segmentation OOD benchmark.

### Cited Findings
- DICOM header metadata is "often filled in incorrectly," so mismatches between expected modality (e.g., T1w) and actual scan modality (e.g., CT or T2w) "may be undetected" — header-based routing alone is not reliable — [Safeguarding AI in Medical Imaging: Post-Hoc Out-of-Distribution Detection with Normalizing Flows](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.11638)
- That 2025 normalizing-flow paper builds graded modality-shift test cases: FLAIR and T1ce scans from the same 75 in-distribution BraTS 2020 test subjects (less drastic shift) plus 150 pre-contrast CT scans from the CQ500 dataset (clear/obvious outlier) — [Safeguarding AI in Medical Imaging](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.11638)
- A multiple sclerosis lesion segmentation study found that OOD detection methods relying purely on the predictive uncertainty of a binary segmentation model "often fail in detecting outlying inputs," whereas learning to segment anatomical labels alongside lesions "highly improves the ability to detect OOD inputs" — [Improving Uncertainty-based Out-of-Distribution detection for medical image segmentation](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2211.05421)
- A subcortical brain structure segmentation study compared deterministic uncertainty methods (DUM, which analyze intermediate network activations) against maximum softmax probability (MSP): DUM outperformed MSP on 6 of 7 OOD test datasets overall, and on 5 of 7 for hippocampus/thalamus-specific segmentation — [Uncertainty-based Quality Control for Subcortical Structures Segmentation in T1-weighted Brain MRI](https://integ04-cnspfv.archimed.fr/doc/OAI_1/oai-HAL-hal-04126201v1)
- The Medical Out-of-Distribution Analysis Challenge (MOOD) 2023 is cited as a benchmark/dataset source for anomaly detection methods that aim to recognize conditions not seen during training without requiring new labels, adaptable to new modalities — [Medical Out-of-Distribution Analysis Challenge 2023](https://zenodo.org/records/7845020)

### Inferences
- For a time-constrained hackathon pipeline, a practical layered approach supported by this literature would be: (1) a cheap image-based sanity check (intensity histogram / simple learned modality classifier) rather than trusting DICOM headers alone; (2) a lightweight post-hoc OOD scorer on model features/activations (DUM-style or normalizing-flow-style) as a second layer, since pure softmax/uncertainty-based detection has documented failure modes; (3) explicit testing against "near-miss" modalities (FLAIR/T2/T1ce confused for T1) rather than only obvious outliers (CT, non-brain images), since the 2025 paper's graded design shows near-misses are the harder case.
- None of the OOD methods found report their own added inference time, so their "lightweight" framing is relative/qualitative (e.g., a classifier or flow model run once per case) rather than independently benchmarked for a 2-minute budget — this should be flagged as an engineering unknown rather than a confirmed fact.

### Gaps
- No source gives a specific wall-clock time or FLOP cost for any of these OOD-detection methods (normalizing flow, DUM, MSP) that would confirm they fit within a 2-minute total inference budget alongside segmentation — this is inferred to be lightweight based on method description only, not measured directly.
- No source was found describing an OOD detector purpose-built and benchmarked for the exact combination of "non-skull-stripped" input detection in a brain MRI pipeline (the retrieved sources cover modality-shift and anomaly/lesion-type shift, but not specifically skull-stripping-state detection) — this appears to be a genuine gap, not just a missed query; a follow-up search specifically on "skull stripping quality control" or "brain extraction verification" could close it if time permits.
