# No tool combines 3D tumor meshes with visible trust

Across clinical products, open-source viewers, academic prototypes, and the broader literature, **no reviewed system — commercial or open-source — combines a lightweight browser-based 3D tumor mesh, linked 2D multi-planar reconstruction (MPR) crosshair views, visible volumetric math, and visible model uncertainty in one place**. Regulated clinical tools (Brainlab Elements, Quantib ND, QyScore, icobrain, Cortechs.ai NeuroQuant, MRIMath i2Contour) deliver validated accuracy but run as desktop workstation plugins or PACS-integrated cloud pipelines that never show uncertainty or step-by-step math to the end user — only inside FDA 510(k) filings that patients and most clinicians never see ([FDA K243633 via Innolitics](https://fda.innolitics.com/device/K243633)). Open-source viewer libraries (NiiVue, OHIF, 3D Slicer, Brainchop) solve visualization but ship no tumor-specific AI; MONAI supplies models but no viewer of its own ([GitHub MONAILabel](https://github.com/Project-MONAI/MONAILabel)). The nearest hackathon-grade prior art — MindView (DeltaHacks 2026) and the unverified Safe Mind/NeuroView project — each implement roughly half of the target feature set and neither reports Dice/sensitivity or shows uncertainty ([devpost.com/software/mindview](https://devpost.com/software/mindview)). This leaves a genuine, well-documented whitespace: a browser tool that treats trust communication — visible confidence, provenance, and fail-safes — as a first-class feature rather than an afterthought, which is exactly where a 24-hour hackathon team can differentiate without having to out-perform BraTS leaderboard architectures.

## Clinical vendors solved accuracy, not transparency

The regulated/commercial landscape splits cleanly into two camps that never overlap. Atrophy/neurodegeneration quantification vendors — **Quantib ND, QyScore, icobrain, Siemens AI-Rad Companion Brain MR** — are all FDA-cleared and CE-marked but explicitly do not segment tumors; their disclosed Dice ranges (Quantib ND: 0.78–0.99 across brain structures, 0.61±0.13 for white-matter hyperintensities) describe dementia-workup structures, not gliomas ([FDA K213737 via Innolitics](https://fda.innolitics.com/device/K213737)). The tumor-specific camp is smaller and newer: **Brainlab's "Elements AI Tumor Segmentation"** module, FDA-cleared under K243633 with mean Dice 0.75 (precision 0.86, recall 0.85) on 412 patients, claims sub-minute segmentation but ships inside a hospital workstation suite with no public interface description ([FDA 510(k) K243633](https://fda.innolitics.com/device/K243633)). Brainlab's older SmartBrush tool, by contrast, is semi-automatic and clinician-guided, with a 2016 PLoS ONE study finding mean Dice 0.72–0.77 across four expert raters versus 0.68 for the fully-automated BraTumIA ([PLoS ONE](https://doaj.org/article/705d1a650f02439999211abfb078f2b8)) — a direct empirical reminder that automated tools and human raters disagree by a comparable margin to inter-rater disagreement itself, which is precisely the kind of fact a trust-communication UI should surface rather than hide. **Cortechs.ai's NeuroQuant** (2025 expansion), **MRIMath's i2Contour** (FDA-cleared, SBIR-funded), and **mediaire's mdbrain Tumor Differentiation module** (CE-marked) round out the tumor-specific commercial field; MRIMath's co-founder describes an interface letting clinicians "review the AI's explanations" alongside volumetric measurements — the single closest documented commercial analogue to a transparency feature, though no detail confirms whether this is a heatmap, text explanation, or something else ([NCI news item](https://www.cancer.gov/about-nci/organization/cbiit/news-events/news/2024/nci-funding-helps-bring-new-ai-aided-device-glioblastoma-market)). Across all nine-plus vendors surveyed, **not one was confirmed to show a live-synchronized, rotatable 3D mesh in a lightweight browser, nor to expose voxel-level uncertainty or step-by-step volumetric math to the end user** — those capabilities exist only inside FDA submissions as static validation numbers, never as an interactive, always-visible product feature. A 2025 retrospective study of an MRIMath-adjacent AI tool found that automated segmentation plus human review detected low-grade glioma growth a median of **21 months earlier** than visual inspection alone — a striking result that underscores how much clinical value sits in catching subtle volumetric change, exactly the kind of number a hackathon demo could make visible and explainable rather than buried in a paper ([PMC12909261](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12909261/)).

## Open-source viewers and models never got wired together

The open-source ecosystem has all the individual pieces but no one has assembled them. **3D Slicer** is the dominant academic platform for interactive segmentation, but it is desktop-only, and its tumor-specific extensions (Slicer-DeepSeg, "Brain-Tumor-Segmentation-on-3D-Slicer") are documented as fragile — user forum reports describe hash-verification failures and crashes on both Ubuntu and Windows 11 ([Slicer Discourse](https://discourse.slicer.org/t/slicer-deepseg-issue/24786)). **MONAI Label** is a model-serving framework with no viewer of its own; its browser path requires wiring together OHIF, an Orthanc DICOMweb server, and NIfTI-to-DICOM conversion — a non-trivial deployment, not a drag-and-drop experience ([MONAI Label release notes](https://pypi.org/project/monailabel/0.2.0)). **NiiVue** is the most promising lightweight browser rendering library (WebGL2, 30+ format support, used in AFNI/FSL Clinical/OpenNeuro) but performs no AI inference and has no confirmed marching-cubes mesh-extraction API of its own ([GitHub niivue/niivue](https://github.com/niivue/niivue)). **OHIF** is the most feature-complete web viewer (MPR, MIP, segmentation rendering, Cornerstone3D-based) and is MONAI Label's official browser integration target, but it too runs no AI itself and needs a DICOMweb backend ([GitHub OHIF/Viewers](https://github.com/OHIF/Viewers)). The single project that gets closest to "drag in a volume, get instant in-browser 3D segmentation" is **Brainchop** — it runs TensorFlow.js inference and NiiVue+three.js rendering entirely client-side with a reported macro Dice of 0.96 — but its pretrained models cover brain masking, gray/white-matter segmentation, and cortical atlases, not tumors, and it operates on a single volume rather than BraTS's four co-registered modalities ([JOSS paper](https://joss.theoj.org/papers/10.21105/joss.05098.pdf)). No open-source repository was found that pairs a BraTS-trained SOTA architecture (nnU-Net, SegResNet, Swin UNETR) with a polished browser viewer, and critically, **no surveyed open-source tool — Slicer, MONAI Label, NiiVue, Brainchop, OHIF, or Papaya — implements volumetric reporting or uncertainty visualization at all**. Several Hugging Face Spaces attempt the exact BraTS-in-browser workflow but are either broken ("Build error," "Sleeping," runtime errors), stubbed (one explicitly "generates demo output" rather than running a real model), or server-side-only rather than true in-browser inference — a pattern of abandonment that itself signals how much harder the full combination is than any individual demo suggests.

## BraTS research sets the accuracy bar a hackathon cannot beat — nor should try to

The BraTS leaderboard lineage is dominated by **nnU-Net** since 2020 and **MedNeXt-based ensembles** since 2023, with winning Dice scores in the high-80s to low-90s (nnU-Net's BraTS 2020 win: Dice 88.95 WT / 85.06 TC / 82.03 ET) achieved through multi-model ensembling and heavy postprocessing rather than single novel architectures ([nnU-Net for Brain Tumor Segmentation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.00848)). Pure transformer models (Swin UNETR, UNETR++) are competitive but not clearly superior and cost more compute — Swin UNETR runs roughly **5–7x slower per case** than a plain 3D U-Net in one benchmark ([Unified Benchmark paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.28858)). The pragmatic lightweight baseline available off-the-shelf — MONAI's pretrained `brats_mri_segmentation` SegResNet bundle, trained only on BraTS 2018's 285 cases — scores an unverified-but-plausible ~85% average Dice on its native dataset, roughly 3–10 points below 2021+ SOTA, and general domain-shift literature implies its real performance on BraTS 2021+ held-out data would likely degrade further, especially in the tumor-core and enhancing-tumor regions ([domain-shift synthesis, arXiv 2212.01235](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.01235)). This matters directly for hackathon strategy: **chasing Dice-score improvements against a 285-case-trained 2018 bundle is a losing and low-value game** given that nnU-Net-class ensembles already define the accuracy ceiling and require compute/engineering far beyond a 24-hour budget. The more tractable research frontier is uncertainty quantification, where the field has moved from expensive multi-pass methods (test-time augmentation, MC-dropout, used in actual BraTS 2017/2020 Challenge entries) toward cheap single-forward-pass alternatives — **evidential deep learning (EDL)**, which parameterizes outputs as a Dirichlet distribution and reports "low computational cost and easy implementation" while matching ensemble-level uncertainty quality on BraTS 2020 ([Li et al., region-based EDL](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10505106/)). A 2025 empirical study is an important caution here: MC-dropout uncertainty correlates only weakly with actual boundary segmentation errors (global r≈0.30–0.38, boundary correlation under 0.05), meaning a naive uncertainty heatmap can look rigorous while communicating little real signal — a trap worth avoiding in any demo that leans on uncertainty as its differentiator ([arXiv 2510.15541](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15541v1)). Ranked by feasibility inside a ~2-minute inference budget, the field's own economics favor, cheapest to most expensive: single-pass EDL, then small flip/rotation-only TTA (2–4 passes), then reduced-N MC-dropout, with full ensembles realistically out of reach for a hackathon GPU budget.

## Safety and trust-UX research gives concrete, buildable design rules

The clearest actionable research cluster for this problem statement is not architecture — it is the human-factors and safety-engineering literature around how uncertainty changes behavior and how pipelines fail safely. A prostate-radiotherapy study found oncologists made **50% fewer manual voxel edits** in regions a model marked as very-low-uncertainty, concluding that showing calibrated uncertainty measurably changes clinical review behavior — direct empirical support for building an uncertainty overlay rather than treating it as decoration ([arXiv 2502.18973](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.18973)). A UCSF meningioma study explicitly calls the lack of calibrated uncertainty "a major barrier to clinical adoption," externally validated on 353 patients ([UCSF news](https://www.ucsf.edu/news/2026/07/432261/can-uncertainty-scores-help-experts-trust-ai-brain-tumor-analysis)). Separately, survey data shows clinicians prefer AI as a "second reader" (75.4% favorable) far more than as a first-reader or autonomous system (26.2% favorable) — a strong argument for a UI that frames every output as a check against human judgment, never a verdict ([CSIRO/Australian Health Review](https://publish.csiro.au/ah/AH23275)). On failure modes, the literature converges on one principle with unusual clarity: **silent failure — the system looks "on" and produces a plausible but wrong output without signaling degradation — is more dangerous than an overt refusal**, and mitigation requires validating orientation/affine metadata at ingestion rather than trusting DICOM/NIfTI headers alone, since headers are "often filled in incorrectly" ([Innolitics engineering guide](https://innolitics.com/articles/medical-imaging-best-practices/); [normalizing-flow OOD paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.11638)). The NIfTI radiological-vs-neurological left-right flip problem is real and documented even in major public research datasets (Functional Connectome Project, OpenfMRI, ABIDE) due to missing or incorrect header information, not just theoretical risk ([Glen et al., *Frontiers in Neuroinformatics* 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroinformatics/articles/10.3389/fninf.2020.00018/epub)). On explainability, Grad-CAM-family methods adapted to 3D segmentation (via the M3d-CAM library) are the most tractable, tooling-supported route for a hackathon, but carry a documented caveat: Grad-CAM "sometimes highlights the wrong location" due to gradient-averaging, so any saliency overlay needs careful framing as suggestive, not causal ([HiResCAM, arXiv 2011.08891](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.08891v3)). One technique stands out as genuinely available but unused as an *interface*: **modality ablation** (removing FLAIR or T1Gd and watching the segmentation change) is well-established as a model-robustness research method, but no paper found frames it as a live, interactive, end-user-facing explanation feature — making an "ablation toggle" a defensible, citeable, low-effort novelty rather than a speculative leap ([missing-modality literature synthesis, arXiv 2509.15017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.15017)). Finally, provenance research (a 2026 chest-X-ray "audit workbench" that SHA-256-hashes datasets, label manifests, and model weights to make reports "tamper-evident and reproducible") has never been applied to 3D tumor segmentation specifically, making a simple model-version/input-hash display panel both low-effort and a genuine first in this sub-domain ([audit workbench paper](https://symposium2026.pcshstrg.ac.th/uploads/projects/234/paper_pdf_20260715_164335.pdf)).

## What's commoditized versus what's actually open

Four things should be treated as solved, off-the-shelf commodities that a hackathon team should consume, not reinvent: (1) **3D browser rendering** — NiiVue or three.js with marching-cubes mesh extraction (via scikit-image or nii2mesh) is mature, well-documented, and already used successfully by both Brainchop and the MindView/Safe Mind hackathon projects; (2) **a usable baseline model** — the MONAI SegResNet BraTS bundle is a legitimate, if dated, inference baseline requiring no training; (3) **the BraTS Dice/HD95 accuracy race** — nnU-Net/MedNeXt ensembles have already won this, and no 24-hour team can meaningfully compete on raw segmentation accuracy; (4) **basic MPR slice viewing** — linked axial/sagittal/coronal views are a 20-year-old, universally expected radiology convention (96.2% of RSNA-surveyed radiologists already used it by 2006), so building one is table stakes, not a differentiator. By contrast, four areas are genuinely open whitespace, each with research-backed, buildable paths: (1) **visible, calibrated uncertainty tied to the 3D view** — pair single-pass EDL or 2–4-augmentation TTA with a heatmap on the mesh itself, directly citing the prostate-radiotherapy finding that this changes review behavior; (2) **an interactive modality-ablation explainability toggle** — repurposing an established research technique into a UI interaction nobody has shipped; (3) **a visible, step-by-step provenance and fail-safe panel** — orientation/affine validation at upload, a skull-strip/brain-sanity heuristic (mask coverage + atlas-overlap threshold), and a model-version/input-hash display, all grounded in concrete literature and all achievable with a few hundred lines of validation code rather than new ML; (4) **explicit "second reader" framing in the UI copy and interaction design itself** — making the tool visibly decline to render a confident-looking mesh when input is invalid or uncertainty is high, rather than silently degrading. These four, combined, would make a hackathon prototype the first found public demo to unify mesh rendering, linked MPR, uncertainty, and provenance in one browser session — a positioning statement directly supported by the research showing no existing project, commercial or open-source, does this.

## Conclusion

The research converges on a counterintuitive strategic insight for a hackathon team under time pressure: the accuracy dimension of this problem is both the most tempting to work on and the least winnable, while the trust/transparency dimension is comparatively unexplored, cheap to implement with existing libraries, and backed by stronger causal evidence of real-world value (the 50%-fewer-edits finding, the "second reader" preference data, the 21-month-earlier detection result). The deepest, most defensible differentiation available in 24 hours is therefore not a better segmentation model but a more honest one — a tool that treats declining to render a confident answer, surfacing calibrated uncertainty on the mesh itself, and showing its own provenance as the actual product, with the segmentation model treated as a commodity component bolted on rather than the centerpiece. That framing also happens to be the one a judging panel can verify in minutes: a toggle that visibly changes the mesh when FLAIR is ablated, an uncertainty color-wash that visibly concentrates at tumor boundaries, and a banner that visibly refuses malformed input are each demonstrable in thirty seconds, which matters as much for a hackathon's limited judging window as for the underlying research case.
